# Voice Anchoring

Ask a language model for "good YouTube copy" and it defaults to good WRITING: dramatic parallels, clever sentence pairs, poetic fragments. None of that is slop vocabulary, so it slides through every slop filter you run, and it still sounds robotic the second a narrator reads it into a microphone, because a voiceover is speech and that register is cinema prose. The one reliable stop is anchoring the script to a real spoken sample before you write a single line. This file is that discipline. Later checks assume it happened.

---

## Pull your anchor

Before writing, pull 60-90 seconds of real spoken narration in the target register. Verbatim, from a transcript, never from memory.

→ **Your own voiceovers first.** If your channel has uploads, grab the transcripts of your last 2-3 openings. Trust the recording over your memory of how you sound, because the remembered version drifts toward the written default.
→ **No uploads yet? Anchor to your niche.** Pull the transcript of a top-performing video in your lane. A finance explainer channel anchors to a winning finance explainer. A history documentary channel anchors to a winning history doc. Same register you are about to write in, proven on a real audience.
→ **Borrow the room, not the person.** No voice of your own yet, and want more than one reference? Pull 2-3 channels you admire in your niche and anchor to the register they SHARE: how they build sentences, where they pause, the energy they carry. You are learning how the lane talks, then saying your own thing in that register. Take what the channels have in common, not what is unique to one of them.
→ **This is voice, not content.** The anchor teaches you how a line SOUNDS, never what it SAYS. Take the shared sentence shapes. Never take one channel's hooks, structure, angles, or topics: that is cloning, `greenlight-audit-skill.md` catches it at Group E, and it is what makes blended scripts read like mush. The register is borrowed. The substance stays yours.
→ **Keep the sample raw.** You want the narration as delivered: the enumerations, the hand-offs, the self-corrections, the filler at its natural density. A cleaned-up version teaches you nothing about shape.
→ **Never write to an imagined "good YouTube voice."** There is no such sample. The imagined version IS the written-cinematic default, so skipping this step guarantees the exact failure this file exists to stop.
→ **No transcript in front of you means no script yet.** If no sample has been provided, stop and ask for one before writing a single line. Never reconstruct a transcript from memory of a video; that puts you right back at the imagined voice.
→ **Anchor in your channel's language.** If your channel is not in English, the sample must be in the language you publish in. An English anchor teaches English sentence shapes, and the shape test only works against the register your viewers actually hear.

What the anchor is for: sentence shapes. How the narration counts things off, how it moves between points, where it breathes.

What the anchor is not for: importing weaknesses. If your old openings start with "hey guys, welcome back," that is a habit to write past, not a shape to copy. Anchor to the register, never to the bad habits inside it.

## The shape test

Run this question on every sentence of spoken copy, no exceptions:

**"Does the narration in your anchor sample ever build a sentence like this?"**

The reference is your pulled sample, not a general idea of good narration. "A real narrator somewhere" is too loose; documentary narrators produce plenty of the exact shapes this test kills, so measuring against "narrators in general" waves them straight through. Hold each sentence against the specific sample you pulled. Judge the shape, not the quality. A trailer line reads clean and scores zero on any slop list, and it still fails this test. Reading well is exactly how it survived every other check.

Shapes that pass, because they are speech:

→ **Enumerations.** "Rent. Insurance. Payroll. That's where the money went." Narrators count things off constantly.
→ **Live-demo narration.** "Watch what happens here." "Okay, now look at the top line." Speech tracks what the viewer is seeing on screen.
→ **Self-corrections.** "It took ten years. Twelve, actually." People fix themselves mid-sentence all the time, and written prose never has to, which is why a self-correction reads as speech the moment you hear it.

Shapes that fail, because they are writing:

→ **Dramatic parallels.** Mirrored beats engineered for the page.
→ **Aphorism pairs.** Two polished sentences built to define each other.
→ **Poetic compression.** A line squeezed until it sounds literary instead of sayable.

Sentence length proves nothing either way. A four-word fragment can be pure trailer voice, and a forty-word ramble can be perfectly spoken. And never wave a line through because it reads polished; polish is the exact failure you are hunting.

If a shape appears nowhere in your anchor sample, rewrite the line into a shape that does.

## The four written-not-spoken tells

These four registers survive slop filters, read-aloud passes, and general QA because nothing in them is banned vocabulary. Learn them by name so you can hunt them. Every example below is the failure, never the guidance.

### 1. Movie Trailer

Escalating parallel beats. The sentence is cut like a teaser, one dramatic clause landing after another.

**The tell:** epic, high-concept STAKES framing and cinematic setup, the "one X, one Y" or "In a world..." grandeur. Scale is the giveaway.

**Written, not spoken:**
```
"One factory closes. Then ten. Then an entire way of life."
```

Read that over b-roll and you are doing a trailer read, not explaining what happened to a town.

### 2. Wise Narrator

Essayist pronouncements. Short verdict sentences delivered like the closing line of a printed essay.

**The tell:** the knowing VERDICT or aphorism, a sentence that pronounces a general truth ("This is not X. This is Y." / "X never Y."). The pronouncement is the giveaway, not the scale.

**Written, not spoken:**
```
"The town never recovered. Towns like this never do."
```

Narrators explain what happened. Verdict sentences belong to essays, and a viewer hears the difference immediately.

### 3. Staccato-Robotic

Fragment cascades posing as impact. Unlike a real enumeration, the fragments do not count actual things; the cascade exists for rhythm alone.

**The tell:** rhythm-only fragments in a drumbeat ("Gears. Springs. Precision.") with nothing being counted. Cadence for its own sake is the giveaway.

**Written, not spoken:**
```
"No warning. No paper trail. No second chances."
```

Test it against the enumeration rule. "Rent. Insurance. Payroll." counts off three real budget entries. The example above is three drumbeats; nothing is being counted. If the fragments count off items you could put on screen as a list, it's an enumeration. If they exist for rhythm, rewrite.

### 4. Reading-Not-Speaking

Clever written pairs and second-person prophecy. Copy that only works on the page, where the eye can admire the symmetry.

**The tell:** written syntax no mouth produces live, subordinate-clause stacks and parenthetical asides, correct on the page and unspeakable aloud. Page-grammar is the giveaway.

**Written, not spoken:**
```
"Attention is the deposit. Retention is the interest."
```

**Written, not spoken:**
```
"You'll never look at a thumbnail the same way again."
```

Try saying either into a microphone as your own sentence, without feeling like you are reciting. The first is a caption. The second tells viewers how they will feel forever, a move no narrator makes unprompted.

## Fix direction

The fix is register translation, never deletion. Three things survive every fix:

→ **The jolt.** Whatever stops the scroll or spikes attention stays.
→ **The proof.** The number, the artifact, the specific detail stays.
→ **The tension.** The open loop the line plants stays planted.

Only the delivery changes.

The anchor governs SHAPE, never SUBSTANCE. You are re-voicing the draft's own claims into your spoken register, not swapping the draft's content for the anchor sample's content. The anchor is where you learn how sentences get built. It is not the source of what the script says.

Concrete test: after the rewrite, every stake, every risk, every hinge moment the draft carried is still on the page, just said plainly. If the draft said everything hinges on a single moment and your rewrite no longer has that stake anywhere, you de-clawed. Put the stake back in spoken words ("one wrong move and the whole piece is done"), do not delete it.

Worked example, using the Movie Trailer line from above.

**Written, not spoken (the original):**
```
"One factory closes. Then ten. Then an entire way of life."
```

**De-clawed (the wrong fix):**
```
"Over the years, a lot of factories in the area ended up closing down."
```

The register is fixed and the line is dead. The escalation is gone, the stakes are gone, nothing pulls the viewer forward. If the fix made the line weaker, the fix is wrong. Go back and translate again.

**Register translation (the right fix):**
```
"That first factory closing was a warning. Within a few years ten more were gone, and by then everyone in town knew the industry wasn't coming back."
```

Same escalation, same stakes, built from shapes a real narrator uses.

The counterweight rule, and it cuts both ways:

→ A line strong enough to beat your niche's proven winners but delivered in trailer voice fails.
→ A line easy to say but flat, with the video's best tension missing from it, fails too.
→ Never downgrade your strongest angle because the spoken version is harder to write. Translate the strongest angle until it is strong AND sayable. That's the whole job.

Smaller rules ride along:

→ **Write contractions in from the first draft.** Spoken copy reads it's / don't / you're / that's, never "it is / do not / you are." Uncontracted forms are written register, not speech, and fixing them later is a re-draft, not a polish.
→ **Filler density is anchored too.** If you loosen a stiff draft with natural fillers ("honestly," "look"), match your anchor's real density. The same filler pasted four times into one script, or into every script you write, becomes a brand-new robotic tic.
→ **Run the test twice.** Anchor before you write, then shape-test the finished draft sentence by sentence during QA. Hooks get held to this hardest, because "viral on paper" is exactly where trailer voice sneaks back in.
