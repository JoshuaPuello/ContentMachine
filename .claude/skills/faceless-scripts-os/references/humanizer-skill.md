# Humanizer (H1-H17)

AI tells rarely arrive one at a time. They cluster: a padded participle clause inside a sentence that inflates its own significance, then a manufactured triad two lines later, then an aphorism to close the section. This file is the catalog of those tells. Learn the seventeen patterns well enough to spot them in a cold read, then apply them the way the last section describes: after drafting, judged in clusters, with the scanner backing you up. Every fenced example below is the failure, never the guidance.

### H1. Superficial "-ing" padding

A present-participle clause tacked onto a finished sentence to fake depth: "highlighting," "underscoring," "showcasing," "reflecting," "ensuring," "symbolizing," "contributing to."

**AI tell:**
```
"The channel uploaded every day for a year, highlighting the importance of consistency and reflecting a broader shift in the niche."
```

**Fix:** cut the clause, or convert it into its own concrete claim with an actor. If the clause describes nothing a viewer could picture, it describes nothing.

### H2. Copula avoidance

"Serves as," "stands as," "functions as," "acts as," "represents," "boasts," "features," "offers" where the sentence means IS or HAS.

**AI tell:**
```
"The video serves as a case study in pacing and boasts a runtime of over twenty minutes."
```

**Fix:** write "is" and "has." ("Represents" survives only when something literally represents something.)

### H3. Significance inflation

The fact arrives wrapped in an importance claim instead of proof. Recognizers: "a pivotal moment," "setting the stage for," "a key turning point," "an evolving landscape," "deeply rooted," "stands as a testament," "reflects broader," "underscores its importance."

**AI tell:**
```
"The upload marked a pivotal moment, setting the stage for the channel's rise and standing as a testament to the evolving landscape of online video."
```

**Fix:** state the fact and delete the wrapper. If the moment mattered, the next sentence proves it with what happened.

### H4. AI vocabulary co-occurrence

One of these words in a natural sentence is nothing. Three in one paragraph is a fingerprint. Judge density, never single words.

**AI tell (the co-occurrence set):**
```
additionally, crucial, pivotal, fostering, garner, interplay, intricate, intricacies,
enduring, enhance, testament, underscore, showcase, vibrant, align with, valuable,
highlight (as a verb), key (as an adjective)
```

**Fix:** swap each for the plain word you would say out loud. Some of the worst offenders sit on the machine tier below and never get written at all, no density call needed:

**Machine-tier subset (see Two-tier bans):**
```
delve, tapestry, unleash, robust, game-changer
```

The landscape metaphor is machine-banned too ("evolving landscape," "digital landscape," and its family), but the bare literal word is fine: a real terrain in a nature, history, or travel video is not a tell.

### H5. Rule-of-three forcing

Triads manufactured for rhythm, usually abstract nouns in parallel.

**AI tell:**
```
"Success in this niche takes patience, persistence, and passion."
```

**Fix:** a real list of three concrete things is fine; narrators count things off constantly. The tell is abstraction-triads recurring section after section. Name the actual items, or keep the one that carries weight.

### H6. Synonym cycling

Rotating labels for the same thing to dodge repetition.

**AI tell:**
```
"The king raised taxes. The monarch ignored every warning. The sovereign had made his last mistake."
```

**Fix:** pick the natural word and repeat it. Repetition is how people talk; the rotation is what sounds generated.

### H7. False ranges

"From X to Y" where X and Y sit on no real scale.

**AI tell:**
```
"His empire stretched from humble beginnings to a legacy that endures today."
```

**Fix:** name the actual items. If you cannot place both ends on the same axis, you don't have a range, so drop the "from X to Y" frame and just state what you mean.

### H8. Subjectless fragments and hidden actors

Actorless or passive constructions that hide who does what.

**AI tell:**
```
"No editing experience required. The captions are generated automatically."
```

**Fix:** give the sentence an actor when active is clearer: "You don't need to know how to edit. The tool writes the captions for you."

### H9. Persuasive authority tropes

Ceremony staged before an ordinary point.

**AI tell:**
```
"At its core, the real question is not how often you upload. Fundamentally, what really matters is the first thirty seconds."
```

**Fix:** cut the ceremony and say the point plainly. Watch the whole family: "in reality," "the deeper issue," "the heart of the matter."

### H10. Aphorism formulas

"X is the Y of Z," "X becomes a trap," "the currency of," "the architecture of." Sentences shaped like quotes waiting for a poster.

**AI tell:**
```
"Consistency is the currency of the algorithm. Comfort becomes a trap."
```

**Fix:** replace it with the concrete claim it gestures at. One earned aphorism at the climax, in a shape your narrator actually produces, can stay. A pattern of them cannot.

### H11. Fake-candor openers

A standalone theatrical pause staged to signal honesty before the reveal.

**AI tell:**
```
"Honestly? Here's the thing. Let's be honest, most creators never check this number."
```

**Fix:** a person being honest just says the thing. A mid-sentence "honestly," at the density your anchor sample shows, is fine; the standalone stage-whisper opener is not speech.

### H12. Filler and stacked hedging

Inflated connective tissue and hedges piled on hedges.

**AI tell:**
```
"In order to understand what happened, it is important to note that the company could potentially possibly have avoided the collapse."
```

**Fix:** "in order to" becomes "to." "Due to the fact that" becomes "because." "At this point in time" becomes "now." "Has the ability to" becomes "can." "It is important to note that" gets cut. Stacked hedges collapse to one.

**H12a. Adverb crutches.** A close cousin of hedging: an "-ly" adverb propping up a weak verb, or one dropped in to fake intrigue.

**AI tell:**
```
"He quietly spoke about the mistake that was simply costing the channel views every single day."
```

**Fix:** collapse the adverb-plus-verb into one precise verb ("quietly spoke" becomes "whispered"), or cut the adverb and confirm the line still stands on its own. Watch "quietly" and "simply" hardest; both get dropped in to manufacture mystery ("quietly costing you views"). Show the thing with a concrete detail instead of dressing a flat verb.

### H13. Generic upbeat closers

The section or script ends on optimism fumes instead of substance.

**AI tell:**
```
"The future looks bright for creators in this niche. Exciting times are ahead."
```

**Fix:** close on a concrete next thing: what happens next, what to watch for, what you will show them. A structured outro usually blocks this at the very end, so the risk zone is the last body section right before the outro; check there.

### H14. Weasel attributions

An authority invoked with no name attached, so the claim can never be checked.

**AI tell:**
```
"Experts argue the algorithm now favors longer videos, and industry reports suggest retention separates the winners from everyone else."
```

**Fix:** name the source or drop the claim. Never invent a source to fill the slot; an unnamed authority persuades nobody and verifies nothing.

### H15. Hyphen and dash uniformity

Two habits travel together: dashes doing every job punctuation has, and hyphens welded onto pairs that don't need them.

**AI tell:**
```
"The niche is competitive — brutally so — and the production is high-quality."
```

**Fix:** a voiceover script gets zero emdashes; a listener cannot hear one, and the model leans on them hard. Rewrite each as a period, a comma, or the word the dash was hiding ("because," "so," "and"). Keep attributive hyphens ("a high-quality mic"); drop predicate ones ("the mic is high quality").

### H16. Signposting announcements

Announcing the content instead of delivering it.

**AI tell:**
```
"Without further ado, let's dive into everything you need to know. Buckle up."
```

**Fix:** do the thing you were about to announce. Most of this family sits on the machine tier below, where no judgment applies. The single carve-out lives there too, quoted in full.

### H17. Trailer voice

The tell that survives every pattern above, because nothing in it is banned vocabulary. Word-for-word copy written like cinema instead of a person talking: it reads clean, scores zero on every slop list, and still sounds robotic the second a narrator reads it aloud.

**AI tell:**
```
"One decision. Three banks. And a fortune that vanished overnight."
```

**Fix:** hunt its four named variants by name: Movie Trailer, Wise Narrator, Staccato-Robotic, and Reading-Not-Speaking. Test every sentence of spoken copy against your anchor sample with the shape test: "Does the narration in your anchor sample ever build a sentence like this?" The full method (the anchor pull, the shape test, the fix direction) lives in `voice-anchoring-skill.md`. The fix is register translation, never de-clawing: the jolt, the proof, and the planted tension all survive; only the delivery changes.

## Two-tier bans

Most of these tells get adjudicated. The machine tier never does, and the split is deliberate, because the two kinds fail differently.

### Machine tier

Absolute. Zero exceptions. Enforced by `trailer-voice-scan.py`, the scanner that ships with this system. A hit in spoken copy is fixed on sight: no deliberation, no "but it fits here." The full list, reproduced from the scanner so you never have to open the code:

**The machine list (never written in spoken copy):**
```
let's dive / let's dive in / let's dive into (any form, including "dive into it")
let's break this down / let's break it down
without further ado
let's unpack
let's jump in / let's jump into
buckle up
here's the kicker
in today's world / in today's day and age
delve
tapestry
unleash
robust
game-changer (any spelling: game changer, gamechanger)
landscape metaphor: evolving / shifting / changing / ever-changing / current / modern / competitive / digital landscape (bare literal "landscape" is fine)
```

The one sanctioned exception: the literal phrase "Let's get into it," at most once per script, and only where it is your channel's earned convention as the hook-to-body pivot. The default is off: if you cannot say for certain the phrase is your channel's established convention, then it is not, and the phrase stays out. Nothing else on the list carries an exception, ever.

The scanner flags more than this list. Its other flags (staccato runs, narrator prophecy, clever written pairs) are judgment-tier candidates for H17: the scanner flags them, you judge each one by the shape test.

### Judgment tier

Everything else in this catalog, H1 through H17. The rule for flagging any of them: quote the specific pattern from this open file when you flag a line. Never rule from memory, because a remembered carve-out drifts wider every time it is invoked, and the drift always lands on the side of keeping the slop.

Two speeds live inside the judgment tier. Some are clean substitutions with no density call to make, so fix them the moment you see them: H6 (pick one word and repeat it), H7 (drop the fake range), H12 (run the swap table), H12a (collapse the adverb crutch), H13 (a script has one ending, so its lone upbeat closer never meets a cluster threshold; catch it on sight), H14 (name the source or cut the claim), and H15 (rewrite the dash, correct the hyphen). One more joins them from a split pattern: the standalone theatrical-opener half of H11 (the staged "Honestly?" pause) gets cut on sight, since one is one too many. H15 in particular is minor and mechanical; fix on sight. The rest are density-judged and only trigger in clusters: H1, H3, H4, H5, H9, H10, and the mid-sentence-candor half of H11. A single instance of one of these is usually noise; a run of them in one section is the fingerprint. H2 and H8 sit in the middle: fix the obvious copula dodge or hidden actor when it reads wrong out loud, but leave a natural sentence alone.

The governing principle, non-negotiable: **a personal verbal tic never licenses a machine-banned phrase.** Quirky tics that are genuinely yours stay on the page ("brother," a signature interjection, an odd greeting that is genuinely yours, not the stock "hey guys, welcome back"). A phrase that is both your tic AND on the machine list never gets written, even if you say it in every upload: on the page it is indistinguishable from generated slop, and you can always ad-lib your own tics at the mic. The tic rule protects your voice; it never overrides the machine list.

Why two tiers exist at all: every layer that reads a script can talk itself into an exception. The writer keeps a banned phrase as "voice," a review pass blesses it from a misremembered rule, and the phrase ships. A regex cannot be argued with. That is the entire point of the machine tier, and it is the reason this system cannot talk itself into slop.

## How to run it

→ **The trigger: "slop check."** Say "slop check" on any draft, any time, and this whole pass runs on demand: the machine list phrase by phrase, the scanner's shape flags by eye, the staccato hunt, the mechanical sweep, then a flag report back in the scanner's format (each hit quoted with its pattern name, HARD-BAN hits marked fix-on-sight, judgment flags marked for the shape test). No Python needed; the lists in this file and the tells in `voice-anchoring-skill.md` are the scanner. This also works on anything that is not a script: a description, a pinned comment, a community post.

→ **After the draft exists, never while writing.** Writing with seventeen patterns held in your head produces stiff, over-corrected copy. Anchor first (`voice-anchoring-skill.md`), draft freely, then sweep.

→ **Machine pass first.** Run `python3 trailer-voice-scan.py your-script.md`. Fix every HARD-BAN hit on sight. Treat its remaining flags as H17 candidates and judge each by the shape test. If the environment cannot run Python (a Claude Project cannot), the pass is not waived: sweep the draft by hand against the machine list in Two-tier bans, phrase by phrase, then hunt the scanner's shape flags by eye.

→ **Judge density-tier tells by clusters, with a concrete threshold.** For the density-judged patterns (H1, H3, H4, H5, H9, H10, and the mid-sentence-candor half of H11), use this trigger so two readers land on the same edit: three hits of the same pattern inside one section, OR two or more different density-tier patterns inside one sentence or a pair of adjacent sentences, means rewrite that passage. Below that, leave it. A lone padded participle, or a single "additionally," carries no verdict on its own. The fix-on-sight patterns from the judgment tier (H6, H7, H11's staged opener, H12, H12a, H13, H14, H15) skip this test; they get corrected wherever they appear.

→ **Don't gut the human parts.** Preserve specific hard-to-fabricate detail, mixed feelings and unresolved tension, era-bound references, varied sentence length, genuine asides and self-corrections, and your real tics (inside the machine-list boundary). Over-editing produces its own tell: every sentence the same length, no opinions, no first person anywhere. A pass that leaves the copy flat has failed even if every pattern is gone.

→ **Run the mechanical sweep too.** The writer at your keyboard is a language model, so its native artifacts are the fastest slop for a viewer to spot. Check for and strip: smart or curly quotes, the ellipsis character, any non-ASCII character, emojis, em and en dashes, a double hyphen "--" standing in for a dash (the substitution a model reaches for once you ban the real one), chatbot artifacts ("I hope this helps," "Would you like me to..."), and knowledge-cutoff disclaimers ("as of my last update," "while details are limited"). These leave no room for judgment; find one, delete it.

→ **The closing loop.** When the sweep is done, reread the whole script and ask one question: "what still reads AI?" Answer it honestly, fix what you named, and ask again. Re-run the scanner after the final fix round and compare the hit counts against your first run: HARD-BAN must read zero, no count may be higher than where it started, and every flag still standing must be an adjudicated false positive (an enumeration, live-demo narration, a self-correction, or a verified tic) with its shape-test verdict noted. Legitimate residue does not get forced down by rewriting protected speech. You are finished only when the honest answer is nothing; never declare a script clean on the first self-report.
