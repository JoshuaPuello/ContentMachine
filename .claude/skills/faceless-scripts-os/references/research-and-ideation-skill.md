# Research & Ideation

Scripts fail at the idea level more often than the writing level. A clean hook on a consensus take still gets skipped, because the viewer has already seen that video on a bigger channel. Everything downstream in this system, the formats, the hooks, the retention work, assumes the idea underneath is worth watching, and until now nothing in the system produced that idea. This file is the step that was missing. Run it before brainstorm, on your own topic and your own niche, and finish with the brief defined at the end. The master workflow picks up from there.

---

## Angle mining

Your first idea on any topic is usually the consensus idea, and the consensus idea is already on the feed with better thumbnails than yours. Angle mining is the discipline of finding the take on a topic that the existing videos do not carry.

Start every pass the same way:

→ **Write the default sentence.** One sentence stating what the average viewer already believes about the topic. "The Titanic sank because it hit an iceberg." "Index funds are the safe boring option." This sentence is the target. Every frame below attacks it from a different side.
→ **Run all four frames against it.** Even when the first frame produces something usable. The second or third pass often beats it.
→ **Pick the winner by proof, not by cleverness.** An angle you cannot source is a thumbnail with no video behind it. The frame that wins is the one whose supporting material actually exists and can be verified in the next section.

### Frame 1: Contrarian

The accepted story is wrong or incomplete, and the record shows it.

→ **The question that generates it:** "What does everyone believe about this that the documents do not support?"
→ **Example line:** "The accepted story says the company died because the market moved on. The filings show it was profitable the year it shut down."

Warning that comes with this frame: contrarian only works when the evidence is real. A contrarian angle without documents behind it is clickbait, and the comments will take it apart in the first hour.

What it looks like when contrarian wins responsibly: the accepted story says the internet killed a famous retail chain, but the public bankruptcy docket shows the chain still ran an operating profit while payments on a decade-old buyout debt drained it. The docket is the receipt; the frame earns the claim instead of just asserting it.

### Frame 2: Hidden-cost

The obvious version of the topic leaves out what it costs. Money, time, risk, or who actually pays.

→ **The question that generates it:** "What does the obvious version of this leave out, and who is paying for it?"
→ **Example line:** "Every video shows the winning trade. None of them show the fee schedule that ate the winnings."

### Frame 3: Untold-story

The history everyone skips. The person, decision, or event inside a famous story that the retellings leave out.

→ **The question that generates it:** "Who or what inside this story does every retelling skip past in one sentence?"
→ **Example line:** "Everyone covers the disaster. Almost nobody covers the operator who saw it coming and spent the last hour trying to stop it."

### Frame 4: Mechanism-reveal

How the thing actually works under the hood. The audience knows the outcome; the video shows the machinery.

→ **The question that generates it:** "Everyone knows WHAT happened. Can I show exactly HOW it happened, step by step?"
→ **Example line:** "You know the phone caught fire. You probably do not know there were two different defects, and the second one was in the fix."

### Picking the winner

→ Run the default sentence through all four questions and write down every angle that fires, even weak ones.
→ Kill any angle that restates the default sentence with stronger adjectives. That is packaging, not an angle.
→ Test each survivor with one question: does this sentence contradict or extend what the viewer already believes? If it only confirms it, the viewer has no reason to click.
→ Rank the survivors by available proof. The next section decides the actual winner, because the frame with the strongest sources wins even when another frame sounded better out loud.

## Sourcing proof for any niche

An angle is a claim. This section is where the claim gets its receipts. Real stories, real numbers, real documents, in any niche, using nothing but a browser.

### Where the material lives

→ **Primary sources.** The document the event itself produced. Company filings, court rulings, patents, government reports, the original study. Whatever everyone else is paraphrasing, go read the thing they are paraphrasing.
→ **Official documents and filings.** Public companies file with regulators (SEC EDGAR is free). Courts publish rulings and dockets (CourtListener is free). Safety agencies publish crash and recall reports (NTSB, FAA, CPSC). Patent databases are open (Google Patents). These are goldmines because almost no creator opens them.
→ **Archives.** Newspaper archives, the Internet Archive and its Wayback Machine, museum and university collections, national archives. For anything historical, the contemporary coverage beats the modern summary every time.
→ **First-hand accounts and primary testimony.** Memoirs, letters, sworn court testimony, oral-history recordings held by archives, on-record conversations published as podcast or broadcast transcripts, earnings-call transcripts, conference talks by the people who were actually in the room. A participant saying it on the record outranks a narrator summarizing it.
→ **Numbers with a named owner.** Every stat was counted by somebody. Central banks, statistics bureaus, industry bodies, the company's own reports. Trace the number to whoever counted it. A stat floating free in a blog post has no owner and no place in your script.

By niche, the pattern repeats:

→ Business and finance → filings, earnings-call transcripts, court records, annual reports.
→ History → archives, digitized newspapers, museum collections, first-hand accounts held by institutions.
→ Tech → regulator recall notices, company newsrooms, patent filings, teardown and lab reports from named outlets.
→ True crime → court records, published rulings, contemporaneous local reporting, sworn testimony.

### The source hierarchy

Primary beats secondary. Secondary beats aggregator. The blog post citing a stat is not the source; the report it links to is. When a chain of citations dead-ends without reaching a document, the stat has no floor under it. Treat it as unverified.

### The verify-or-cut rule (hard rule)

**Every stat and every story either traces to a source you actually opened, or it does not go in the script.**

→ Not a source a compilation video mentioned. Not a number you remember reading somewhere. Opened, read, logged.
→ Log the source next to the item the moment you verify it. The PROOF BANK format below has a slot for this. An item without its source line is not verified, it is pending.
→ If verification fails, the item is cut, and no later step gets to resurrect it because the video "needs a number there."
→ Fabricated numbers never ship. Not as placeholders you meant to fix, not as round estimates dressed as data. If a fact is needed but not yet verified, hold it in the brief as a marked placeholder, like `[stat you verified + where]`, and either fill it or cut the line that needed it before writing.

This rule is also what gets your script through the system's own quality gates. The master workflow's anti-slop scan hunts suspicious numbers and kills them. A proof bank built this way is the reason your numbers survive that scan: they are real, they are specific, and you can say where they came from.

## Cold-niche research

You picked a niche you know nothing about, or you are evaluating one before committing. This is the repeatable path from zero knowledge to a working map, using only tools you already have: YouTube search, video transcripts, Google, and primary sources. Budget 1-2 focused hours, not a week.

### Step 1: Map the outlier videos

→ Search the niche's core terms on YouTube. Plain searches first ("submarine disasters", "dividend investing", "budget gaming laptop"), then variations a beginner would type.
→ For every promising result, open the channel page and compare that video's views to the channel's normal. To pin down the normal, take the median views of the channel's last 10-15 long-form uploads, ignoring Shorts and anything under about a week old. A channel whose uploads sit around 20K views with one video at 400K is showing you an outlier: a video that pulled far beyond what the subscriber base could supply. That gap is demand, measured for free.
→ A working threshold: 5x or more above the channel's typical recent video. The multiple matters more than the raw view count. Two million views on a ten-million-subscriber channel proves nothing; 300K on a channel that normally does 15K proves the topic and packaging did the work.
→ Collect 10-15 outliers across several channels. Log title, channel, views, and the channel's normal range.

### Step 2: Reverse the demand from titles

→ For each outlier, write down the promise its title and thumbnail make, in plain words. "You will find out why this famous thing failed." "You will see what this costs before you buy it." "You will learn the thing insiders know."
→ Group the promises. Three or four clusters will appear. Those clusters are the niche's proven demand, reverse-read from what the audience already clicked at scale.

### Step 3: Read the openings

→ Pull the transcripts of the top 5 outliers. Almost every YouTube video has an open transcript panel (description → "Show transcript"), so this costs nothing. If one has no transcript, take the next outlier down your list.
→ Study the first 60-90 seconds of each: what tension gets planted, what proof appears and how early, what gets promised for later.
→ Note the register while you are in there. If you will write in this niche, these transcripts double as anchor material for the voice-anchoring skill.

### Step 4: Extract the proven patterns

→ Formats that repeat across outliers (documentary, listicle, explainer, breakdown).
→ Angles that repeat. Run the four frames from Angle mining backwards: which frame is each outlier using? Niches usually lean on one or two and ignore the rest.
→ Proof types that repeat: archival footage, on-screen documents, screen-recorded data, first-hand accounts.

### Step 5: Find the gap

The outliers show what works. The gap is what all the top performers skip.

→ Read the comments on the top outliers. Questions that keep appearing with no video answering them are unmet demand written down for you.
→ Note the step every video glosses over in one sentence. That sentence is often a full video.
→ Note the claims nobody sources. A niche where every video repeats the same unsourced number is a niche where one properly sourced video takes the authority position.
→ Check which of the four frames the niche ignores. A niche that only does untold-story is open at mechanism-reveal, and the reverse.

### Step 6: Source before you commit

→ Take your best gap-angle to Google and the primary sources from the previous section. Spend 20-30 minutes confirming the material exists: the documents, the numbers, the story details.
→ If the proof is there, you have a video, and most of your PROOF BANK is already collected.
→ If the proof is not there, drop back to Step 5 and take the next gap. Committing to an unprovable angle wastes the entire downstream pipeline.

## The output contract

This process ends in a brief. Not notes, not a folder of tabs, a brief in this exact format. **The master workflow reads this brief at STEP 0, before anything else runs. The field names are the interface between this file and the rest of the system. Do not rename them.**

```
TOPIC: [the subject, stated in words a viewer would actually search]
ANGLE: [one sentence: the winning frame + the non-obvious take it produced]
PROOF BANK:
→ [verified item 1 + the source you opened]
→ [verified item 2 + the source you opened]
→ [continue: 5-8 items total, every one carrying its source]
HOOK DIRECTION: [which proof item leads + the tension it plants]
STRUCTURE HINT: [the format the material wants + one clause on why]
```

What goes in each field, and what done looks like:

→ **TOPIC** = the subject in plain searchable words. Done when a stranger could paste it into YouTube and land in the right territory.
→ **ANGLE** = one sentence naming the frame and the take. Done when the sentence contradicts or extends what the viewer already believes, instead of restating it.
→ **PROOF BANK** = 5-8 verified items, each with its source on the same line. Done when every item has passed verify-or-cut and nothing is pending except clearly marked placeholders you intend to fill before writing.
→ **HOOK DIRECTION** = the single strongest proof item and the open question it plants. Done when hook construction could start from this line alone, with no new research.
→ **STRUCTURE HINT** = the format this material naturally fits: documentary, listicle, exposé, true crime, video essay, or explainer. Video essay and explainer have no structure template of their own in the master workflow, so when the hint is one of those, also name the borrowed spine from the four templated formats (documentary, listicle, exposé, true crime), as in "explainer with a documentary spine". Done when the hint names a format the master workflow has a template for, directly or as the named spine.

How the brief maps onto the existing pipeline:

→ TOPIC and STRUCTURE HINT feed STEP 1 (Identify Format).
→ ANGLE and HOOK DIRECTION feed STEP 2 (Hook Construction); the hook patterns there expect specific names and numbers, which is exactly what HOOK DIRECTION hands over.
→ STRUCTURE HINT feeds STEP 3 (Structure Templates).
→ PROOF BANK feeds the body writing throughout, and it is the reason the finished script passes the anti-slop scan's real-numbers check instead of failing it.

A brief with an empty or padded PROOF BANK is not done. Go back to sourcing or change the angle.

## Worked examples

Three niches, run from blank topic to finished brief. Every fact below is either a placeholder you fill from the named source before writing, or a qualitative claim tied to a real openable document, and at runtime there is no famous-fact exemption on top of that: any item not traced to a source actually opened in this session goes in brackets, no matter how sure you are. And if sources cannot be opened in this chat at all, every factual item ships as a bracketed placeholder for the buyer to fill before writing. A brief full of placeholders is a valid brief; a brief full of unverified facts is not.

### Example 1: Finance explainer

**Starting topic:** "something about hedge funds vs index funds"

**The angle-mining pass.** Default sentence: "Hedge funds are what rich people use to beat the market." Contrarian fires but lands on consensus: "index funds actually win" is already the standard finance-channel take, so it fails the contradict-or-extend test. Mechanism-reveal produces "how 2-and-20 fees actually compound", workable but abstract. **Hidden-cost wins:** the obvious version of hedge funds leaves out that the fee structure pays managers even when performance disappoints, and there is a famous, fully documented public bet that made this visible. Strong frame plus strong proof beats the cleverer-sounding option.

**Proof bank, first entries:**

→ Warren Buffett publicly bet $1 million in 2008 that a plain S&P 500 index fund would beat a hand-picked basket of hedge funds over ten years; the asset management firm Protégé Partners took the other side. Source: Berkshire Hathaway shareholder letters, free on the company site, which recap the bet directly.
→ The bet ran 2008 through 2017 and the index fund won; the winnings went to the charity Girls Inc. of Omaha. Source: Berkshire's 2017 shareholder letter, plus the bet's own page on Long Bets (longbets.org), which names the charity.
→ `[the exact ten-year returns for both sides, from the 2017 Berkshire letter]`
→ `[a typical hedge fund fee structure vs an index fund expense ratio, from a source you verified]`

**The brief:**

```
TOPIC: Warren Buffett's $1 million bet against the hedge fund industry
ANGLE: Hidden-cost. The famous bet was never really about stock picking; it was a ten-year public demonstration that fees, not performance, decide who gets rich from a hedge fund.
PROOF BANK:
→ Buffett bet $1M in 2008 that an S&P 500 index fund beats a basket of hedge funds over ten years; Protégé Partners took the other side (Berkshire Hathaway shareholder letters)
→ The bet ran 2008-2017; the index fund won; winnings went to Girls Inc. of Omaha (Berkshire 2017 letter + the bet's page on Long Bets)
→ [exact ten-year returns for both sides, from the 2017 Berkshire letter]
→ [typical 2-and-20 hedge fund fee structure vs index fund expense ratio, from a verified source]
→ [2 more verified items: e.g. what the stake was invested in as the bet ran, from the letters]
HOOK DIRECTION: Lead with the wager itself: the most famous investor alive publicly bet $1 million that doing nothing beats the smartest money managers in the world, and the managers took the bet. Tension planted: how badly did it go for them, and why did the outcome have almost nothing to do with picking stocks?
STRUCTURE HINT: Explainer with a documentary spine. The bet gives a ten-year chronology to hang the fee mechanics on, so teach inside the story rather than lecturing first.
```

### Example 2: History documentary

**Starting topic:** "the Halifax Explosion"

**The angle-mining pass.** Default sentence: "A ship exploded in Halifax harbour in 1917 and flattened the city." Contrarian has nothing; the record and the accepted story agree. Hidden-cost is thin. Mechanism-reveal is viable (how a slow harbour collision became the largest man-made blast of the pre-atomic era) and would make a solid video. **Untold-story wins:** nearly every retelling spends its runtime on the blast and gives one sentence to Vince Coleman, the train dispatcher who understood what was about to happen and spent his final minutes telegraphing a warning to an incoming passenger train. A named person, a documented decision, and a clock make a stronger spine than physics alone, and the archival proof exists.

**Proof bank, first entries:**

→ On December 6, 1917, the French cargo ship SS Mont-Blanc, loaded with wartime explosives, collided with the Norwegian vessel SS Imo in Halifax harbour; the resulting explosion destroyed much of the city's north end and killed close to 2,000 people. Source: the Nova Scotia Archives' online Halifax Explosion collection and the Canadian Encyclopedia entry on the Halifax Explosion, both free to open.
→ It stands as the largest man-made explosion before the development of atomic weapons. Source: the Maritime Museum of the Atlantic's Halifax Explosion exhibit pages and the same Canadian Encyclopedia entry.
→ Vince Coleman, a railway dispatcher, stayed at his post to telegraph a warning to stop an incoming passenger train and was killed in the blast; his final message is part of the documented record. Source: the Nova Scotia Archives' Halifax Explosion collection and the Maritime Museum of the Atlantic's exhibit pages.
→ `[the wording of Coleman's final telegraph message as held by the Nova Scotia Archives; versions differ, so quote the archival one]`

**The brief:**

```
TOPIC: the Halifax Explosion of 1917
ANGLE: Untold-story. Everyone covers the blast; this video follows the train dispatcher who saw it coming, chose to stay, and spent his last minutes sending the warning that stopped an incoming passenger train.
PROOF BANK:
→ Dec 6, 1917: SS Mont-Blanc, carrying wartime explosives, collided with SS Imo in Halifax harbour; close to 2,000 killed (Nova Scotia Archives Halifax Explosion collection + Canadian Encyclopedia entry)
→ Largest man-made explosion before atomic weapons (Maritime Museum of the Atlantic exhibit pages + Canadian Encyclopedia entry)
→ Vince Coleman, railway dispatcher, stayed to telegraph a warning to an incoming train and died in the blast (Nova Scotia Archives Halifax Explosion collection + Maritime Museum of the Atlantic)
→ [wording of Coleman's final message, quoted from the Nova Scotia Archives version]
→ [timeline of the ~20 minutes between collision and detonation, from the Maritime Museum of the Atlantic's account]
→ [what happened to the incoming train and its passengers, from the Nova Scotia Archives collection]
HOOK DIRECTION: Lead with Coleman at his telegraph key: one man watching a burning ship drift toward the pier, fully understanding what the cargo means, with an incoming train full of passengers and minutes to decide. Tension planted: he could have run, and the message he sent instead is still in the record.
STRUCTURE HINT: Documentary, cold-open on the minutes before the blast, then step back to build the collision chronologically. The true crime cold-open structure fits even though this is history: most dramatic moment first, then the setup.
```

### Example 3: Tech review channel

**Starting topic:** "Samsung Galaxy Note 7 retrospective"

**The angle-mining pass.** Default sentence: "The Note 7 was the phone that caught fire and got recalled." Untold-story is thin here; the story was covered live by the entire tech press. Contrarian ("the Note 7 was actually a great phone") is a take, not a video, and it fights the record. Hidden-cost overlaps with what every recap already says about the money. **Mechanism-reveal wins:** everyone knows the phone burned, almost nobody can explain why, and the real mechanism is genuinely surprising: there were two different battery defects from two different suppliers, and the second defect was in the replacement units shipped as the fix. The company's own engineering post-mortem is public, which makes this the rare angle where the primary source is also the most dramatic document.

**Proof bank, first entries:**

→ Samsung recalled the Galaxy Note 7 in September 2016 after battery fires; replacement units also caught fire, forcing a second recall and the permanent discontinuation of the phone in October 2016. Source: Samsung's own newsroom statements and the US Consumer Product Safety Commission recall notices, both public.
→ In January 2017, Samsung's public post-mortem identified two separate battery defects, one in each of the two suppliers' batteries: the original battery and the replacement battery failed for different reasons. Source: Samsung's January 2017 newsroom announcement, still online.
→ US authorities banned the phone from all flights, with airline gate announcements naming a single consumer product. Source: the US Department of Transportation emergency order of October 2016, published on the department's site.
→ `[number of units recalled, from the CPSC recall notice]`

**The brief:**

```
TOPIC: why the Galaxy Note 7 actually caught fire
ANGLE: Mechanism-reveal. Everyone remembers the fires; the real story is that two different defects from two different suppliers failed in two different ways, and the second one was inside the phones shipped as the fix.
PROOF BANK:
→ Sept 2016 recall after battery fires; replacements also burned; phone permanently discontinued Oct 2016 (Samsung newsroom, CPSC recall notices)
→ Jan 2017 post-mortem: two separate defects, one per battery supplier, with different failure modes (Samsung January 2017 announcement)
→ US DOT emergency order banned the phone from all flights, Oct 2016 (published DOT order)
→ [number of units recalled, from the CPSC notice]
→ [the specific failure mode of each battery defect, from the post-mortem]
→ [what the recall cost Samsung, from its investor disclosures for those quarters]
HOOK DIRECTION: Lead with the flight ban: airlines were naming one specific phone in gate announcements, the only consumer product to earn that. Tension planted: the first fires were not the strange part; the strange part is that the officially safe replacements started burning too, which meant the diagnosis itself was wrong.
STRUCTURE HINT: Explainer on a documentary spine, built as a two-act mechanism reveal: act one is the first defect and the recall that was supposed to fix it, act two is the replacement fires and what the post-mortem actually found. The BUT/THEREFORE chain is already in the events.
```

### What the three examples share

→ Each default sentence was written first, and each winning frame beat at least one better-sounding rival because its proof existed.
→ Every proof item carries its source on the same line, and every unfetched fact sits in a marked placeholder instead of a made-up number.
→ Each brief hands the master workflow a hook it can build without new research, and a structure it does not have to guess.

That is the full pass. Finish the brief before asking for a script, then hand it to the master workflow, which can build from it without doing any new research.
